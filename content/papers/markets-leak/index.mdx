Three weeks to the US election. The polls say coin flip, 50-50 inside the margin of error, as they've said for months. Prediction markets say something else: Polymarket has drifted to roughly 60-40 for Trump, hundreds of millions in cumulative volume behind it. So the discourse picked its fighter: "a whale is manipulating the odds". Maybe. It's the wrong question. The right question is what each number actually measures.

## Polls herd, measurably

A poll is an instrument wrapped in incentives. Response rates sit under 1%, so every published number is mostly weighting model, not raw sample. And pollster incentives point inward: publish an outlier and be wrong, you're the industry joke; publish the consensus and be wrong, everyone was wrong together. Nate Silver [proved the effect statistically](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/heres-proof-some-pollsters-are-putting-a-thumb-on-the-scale/) a decade ago, and the method is the beautiful part, because it's a variance floor argument. Random sampling *forces* a minimum spread: two honest polls of 800 people each should disagree by about 3,5 points on average from noise alone, before any real methodological differences add more. When the late-October polls of a race cluster tighter than that mathematical floor, someone is nudging numbers toward the pack; the spread that should exist has been suppressed by hand. Herding isn't an accusation, it's an inequality violated. A 50-50 that every pollster reports is not information. It's insurance.

Markets invert the incentive: being right against consensus is the only way to profit. Nobody gets paid for agreeing with everyone.

## This is the older technology, by a century

The framing "markets are the weird new alternative to polls" has history exactly backwards. Rhode and Strumpf [documented](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0895330042162340) the enormous election betting markets that ran on Wall Street from the 1880s to the 1930s: in peak weeks the betting turnover exceeded trading on the stock exchange itself, newspapers printed the odds as news, and the prices were embarrassingly accurate, calling every clear winner from 1884 to 1940 except the photo-finish of 1916. What killed them wasn't inaccuracy. It was scientific polling arriving in 1936 as a cheaper information substitute, plus legal gambling alternatives. Gallup didn't beat the markets; he undercut their business model. So the 2024 question isn't whether betting can price elections, that ran in production for fifty years. It's whether *these* markets, with *this* liquidity, do.

## We've run the manipulation experiment too

The whale question isn't hypothetical, and it has a body count. In 2012, one Intrade trader spent the final two weeks buying Romney contracts, losing an estimated **4 to 7 million dollars**, at times a third of all Romney volume, apparently to keep the race looking close. Rothschild and Sethi [dissected all ~84.000 transactions](https://rajivsethi.blogspot.com/2013/09/the-romney-whale.html) afterward: the whale built a price "firewall", Obama capped near 70%, Romney floored near 30%, and the wall held right until Ohio was called, at which point reality marked his book to zero.

Note what that episode proves, in both directions. Yes, a determined actor can distort a thin market for a while: the number you read carries that risk, and today's discourse about a large Polymarket account is a legitimate version of it. And simultaneously, every cent of distortion is someone else's subsidized arbitrage. Push a 50% probability to 60% and you're handing out free expected value to everyone who disagrees; the experimental literature (Hanson, Oprea and Porter ran it in the lab) finds manipulation attempts get absorbed with little lasting price impact, because informed traders eat the manipulator. Robin Hanson pushed the logic one step further with his subsidized market makers: if noise and manipulation *pay* informed traders to show up, a manipulator is functionally a philanthropist funding price discovery. Elections resolve. The tape you painted gets marked to truth in November. Herding, meanwhile, is free, invisible, and career-positive. Pick your poison, but only one of them has an immune system.

## Markets don't lie, they leak

The strongest property of a prediction market isn't wisdom-of-crowds arithmetic. It's the one [Wolfers and Zitzewitz formalized](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0895330041371321): a market is the one public number **private information cannot resist touching**. A campaign's internal polling, a fund's commissioned surveys, an insider's nervous certainty: none of that reaches a pollster's weighting model. All of it reaches a market, because sitting on tradeable information costs money every hour you sit on it. The price is a leak aggregator.

Three honest caveats before you treat the price as gospel:

1. **Price is not the crowd's mean belief.** Manski [showed](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10359) that with heterogeneous beliefs and budget constraints, a 60% price only bounds the mean belief inside a wide interval; Wolfers and Zitzewitz later gave conditions where price ≈ mean belief, but the equality is an assumption, not a theorem. Read 60% as a region, not a decimal.
2. **The favorite-longshot bias is real.** Betting markets systematically overprice longshots and underprice favorites at the extremes; a 5% contract is usually worth less than 5%. At 60-40 the bias is small, but it exists, and it says cheap-looking tails are cheap for a reason.
3. **Thin books leak noise along with information.** Polymarket's election book is deep by crypto standards and shallow next to anything a bond desk would call liquid. Depth is the exchange rate between dollars and distortion.

And one caveat for the other side: markets are partly *parasitic* on polls. Watch the tape after a big poll drops; the price moves, because polls are an input to traders. The two instruments aren't independent witnesses, they're a food chain. Polls still do things markets can't: crosstabs, state-level structure, the *why* behind the number. The market compresses everything into one probability; the poll tells you which voters moved. You want both, and you want to know which one is copying the other's homework this week.

So read the 60-40 the way you'd read any leak: not as truth, but as evidence that someone, somewhere, is betting real money that the polls are herding again. A probability is not a prediction: the losing side of 60-40 should win four times in ten, and when it happens the "markets were wrong" takes will write themselves. In three weeks we get one draw from the distribution. The polls will explain themselves afterward. The market already committed its statement.
