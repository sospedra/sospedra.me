Every engineering leader I talk to says the same two sentences, usually in the same breath. "We only hire seniors now." And: "it's so hard to find seniors." Nobody laughs. The joke is structural, so it doesn't register as one: **seniors are not found, they're manufactured, and every company just closed its factory.**

## The data stopped being anecdotal

Stanford economists ran ADP payroll microdata, actual employment records for millions of workers, and [found the canaries](https://bharatchandar.substack.com/p/a-primer-on-canaries-in-the-coal): workers aged 22-25 in the most AI-exposed occupations are down 13% in relative employment since late 2022, controlling for firm-level shocks. For young software developers specifically, headcount fell nearly 20% from the late-2022 peak. Workers over 30 in the same occupations: flat to growing. Two details in the paper matter more than the headline. The adjustment came through employment, not wages, which is what you'd expect when the marginal junior is simply not hired rather than repriced. And it concentrated where AI *automates* rather than augments; where the models assist instead of replace, young employment held.

The hiring-side data cuts the same shape. SignalFire's [talent report](https://www.signalfire.com/blog/signalfire-state-of-talent-report-2025) has new grads at 7% of Big Tech hires in 2024, down a quarter year over year and over half versus 2019, while mid and senior hiring *rebounded* in the same period. The market recovered; the bottom rung didn't. Zoom out and the whole ladder is shorter: Indeed's software postings index [peaked at 233](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IHLIDXUSTPSOFTDEVE) in February 2022 (Feb 2020 = 100) and sits around 65 today, and "software engineer" titles are down 49% from pre-pandemic. Meanwhile the [NY Fed's tables](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market) show recent CS grads at 6,1% unemployment and computer engineering at 7,5%, against 5,8% for all recent graduates. Read that again: the majors sold to a generation as the safe bet now run *above* the average that includes art history. The safest rung and the bottom rung turned out to be the same rung.

## What the intern actually paid with

The apprenticeship deal was never charity. A junior was cheap labor at the bottom of the task ladder, and the grunt work was the tuition. This arrangement is genuinely old and genuinely studied: [Epstein's work](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/22419/) on medieval guilds shows apprenticeship existed to solve a market failure in training investment. Masters trained only because enforceable multi-year contracts let them recoup the cost from the apprentice's later productive years; the apprentice paid with underpriced labor, the master paid with knowledge, and the contract kept either side from defecting early. England thought this important enough to *legislate*: the Statute of Artificers made a completed seven-year apprenticeship a legal requirement to practice most trades, [from 1563 until 1814](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102783/1/Wallis_apprenticeship_in_england_accepted.pdf). There's even a respectable case ([de la Croix, Doepke and Mokyr](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22131)) that this institution, dense person-to-person transmission of tacit skill between non-relatives, is a real chunk of why pre-industrial Europe pulled ahead of everyone else. Skills transmission is not a perk. It's infrastructure, and it has always needed scaffolding, because it has never paid for itself on a quarterly ledger.

Then the machine started doing the grunt work, and the tuition currency collapsed. An LLM produces intern-quality output on intern-shaped tasks without onboarding, equity, or a manager's Tuesday. The seats stopped making quarterly sense, so the seats went away.

## The economics has a name, and it isn't laziness

Here's the part the "companies are short-sighted" take misses: this outcome is *individually rational*, which is exactly what makes it collectively stupid. Economists have known since [Pigou in 1912, formalized by Becker](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6740), that in a frictionless labor market firms refuse to pay for general training: train someone in portable skills and a competitor who trained nobody poaches them with the salary you can't match, because you're still amortizing the training. The poaching externality. So why did tech firms train juniors anyway, for seventy years? [Acemoglu and Pischke's answer](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6357): frictions. Switching jobs was costly, information about outside offers was bad, wages were compressed, so general skills behaved like firm-specific ones and the investment quietly paid back.

Now run the 2025 update, term by term. Remote work made switching nearly frictionless. Salary transparency and levels.fyi killed the information asymmetry. And AI zeroed out the junior's first-year output, the thing that used to offset training cost while the judgment grew. Every force pushes toward the Becker corner: the equilibrium where training is nobody's rational move and everybody's necessity. This isn't a moral failure by CFOs. It's a market failure, the textbook kind, the kind that doesn't fix itself by exhortation.

## The objections, taken seriously

Two pushbacks deserve airtime. The first: *AI is the best tutor a junior ever had*, so maybe the pipeline doesn't shrink, it accelerates; a motivated 23-year-old with an agent learns in months what took years. Partly true, and it misses what seniority is made of. The tutor teaches skills. Judgment grows by *owning consequences*: shipping the migration that pages you at 3am, defending the estimate that slipped, unwinding your own bad abstraction a year later. AI compresses skill acquisition and does nothing for consequence acquisition, and companies just removed the seats where consequences came in junior sizes. The second objection: *this is just the hiring cycle*, 2022 was a bubble and everything reverted. The SignalFire asymmetry answers that one: senior hiring recovered, new-grad hiring kept falling through the recovery. A cycle sinks all boats. This dropped one deck.

And the lead time makes the bill invisible until it's enormous. Seniority takes five to ten years to grow. The cohort not hired in 2023-2025 is the missing staff engineers of 2032 and the missing principals of 2035, arriving exactly as the seniors everyone exclusively hires start aging out. Demography is the slowest, surest form of technical debt.

## What restocking actually looks like

The last time skills transmission faced this incentive structure, the answer wasn't virtue, it was institutions, and the existing ones are instructive. Medicine doesn't ask hospitals to train doctors out of civic feeling; residency is a *funded, mandatory* apprenticeship, and it survives precisely because no hospital can skip it and poach instead. Germany's dual system splits the bill between firms and the state and keeps youth unemployment structurally low. The UK tried an [apprenticeship levy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/apprenticeship-levy) in 2017: clumsy, gamed, and directionally correct, a tax on payroll refunded to whoever actually trains. The software version writes itself in some combination: agent-era residencies where juniors own small blast-radius systems with real consequences and AI does the typing; training treated as infrastructure spend with retention contracts doing what guild indentures did; and, if the free-riding persists, someone will eventually reach for the levy. The market failure is textbook; so are the remedies.

If you're the junior in this story, the advice compresses to one line: manufacture your own consequences. Own something end to end anywhere that will let you, an OSS package with real users, a side project with real invoices, the unglamorous internal tool nobody else wants, because ownership is the input the market stopped providing and still pays for.

The companies harvesting seniors trained on someone else's payroll are running the oldest free-rider play in economics, at the exact moment the pool stopped refilling. Ask them, in 2035, who trained their seniors. Someone did. It just won't have been them.
