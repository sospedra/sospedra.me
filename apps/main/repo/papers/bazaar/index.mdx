On the night of July 28, 2026, thirty renders of the same market stall landed in my review gallery, and all thirty were wrong the same way. The order asked for a hacker den: Ed from Cowboy Bebop cross-legged on a rug, one lit CRT, racks of dead machines behind. The renders came back with the camera dropped, the rug bloated into a stage, the furniture rotated inward around the character. I asked the supervisor one question: how do you fail thirty times out of thirty? The audit answered it. The shared order body carried the poison: "camera slightly LOWER", "the rug band is DEEP", "racks ROTATED TOWARD Ed, enclosing him". The model had obeyed perfectly, thirty times in a row. **The order was the bug.**

![The thirty stall-war masters, all rejected. Every one obeyed the same poisoned order.](./stall-war-30.webp)

That stall belongs to [the bazaar](/bazaar), the pixel-art night market that is now this site's index. Eight stalls, one per section, run by keepers who talk. Every pixel was generated by gpt-image-class models driven through Codex CLI, supervised by Claude, art-directed by me. Twelve days, five rebuilt routes, 311 archived order files, 230 shipped assets.

The method earned a name along the way: **adversarial art direction**. Every order faces a hostile review before it flies. Every landing faces a ruler. Every approval survives an attempt to refute it. GANs make the adversary a loss function; this pipeline makes it a reviewer with taste. What follows is the build log: the pipeline, the wars it fought, the layout system underneath, and the work the models still cannot do.

## A market instead of a menu

The site needed an index that was not a list of links. The answer is a place: an underground market where every stall is a route and navigation is wandering. The reference cocktail is exact. [Final Fantasy IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_Fantasy_IX) item shops supply the interaction grammar: hover dialogs, typewriter text, shopkeeper personality. Neo-Tokyo supplies the mood: neon, rust, cable clutter. 16-bit market scenes supply the rendering: chunky color, outlined sprites, flat depth.

The mapping rule: **the stall's fiction rhymes with the route's content**. The console stall is a hacker den because /console is a terminal. The talks stall is a video club because talks are tapes. The w98 stall grows plants because /w98 is a living old machine. The games kids argue about who plays because the games are two-player-sibling energy.

  - **w98** · a gardener robot watering the racks

  - **travel** · a small alien, "Supernova in twenty-two. Coming?"

Characters carry the whole personality budget. Each stall has exactly one focusable interaction, the dialog types at *9ms per character* (`TYPEWRITER_INTERVAL_MS = 9`), and links type in last so the eye lands on them. The market is vertical: a street on top sells the fiction with a bus stop back to the city, a neon sign, a door that opens. Below it, market floors chained by spiral stairs. You scroll down into the underground.

## Three roles, one law

The pipeline has three roles, strictly separated. The **art director** rules by eye, supplies reference images, and never writes prompts. The **supervisor** writes orders, flies fleets, verifies mechanically, and reports with numbers. The **contractor** executes one order per job: talented, unreliable, forgetful. The whole pipeline exists to compensate for that third adjective.

<Pull>Never trust the contractor.</Pull>

The first law: **never trust the contractor**. Every landing gets mechanical verification: canvas dimensions, art bounding box, chroma coverage, hash uniqueness across concurrent jobs, measured angles. *A ruler catches what an eye forgives.* The model claims nothing. The ruler decides.

Fleets fly detached, one status file per job, a watcher polling for landings:

```bash
nohup bash -c "codex exec ... - < order.md ; echo EXIT=$? >> status" & disown
```

The order goes through stdin because positional `"$(cat)"` arguments silently vanish inside `nohup bash -c`. Concurrent jobs sharing a working directory collide on image-gen temp files: two jobs once returned **byte-identical PNGs**, caught only by md5. The fix is per-job directory isolation, a random launch stagger, and absolute output paths inside every order. One more physical constant: the model renders 1536x1024 and 1024x1536 exactly, and its "square" is a 1254x1254 preset. Ask for anything else and you get a preset anyway.

![An early whole-floor master, round 1. The campaign starts here: one image, every mistake still undiscovered.](./floor-master-r1.webp)

Every round ends the same way: the fleet lands into an HTML gallery, captions carry the verdict numbers, references sit at the top, and the art director judges in one tab. Fleets fly detached; approval never does. **Nothing self-approves.**

## The anatomy of an order

An order is a markdown file with four parts, and every part is scar tissue from a recorded failure.

1. **Attachments with roles.** "Image 1 is the composition plan: all invisible constraints. Obey the layout, never draw any of it." The never-draw clause is blood law because models otherwise paint the attached diagram into the art. It happened.
2. **Canvas and chroma contract.** Flat `#00ff00` outside the art, explicitly scoped: background key only, zero art pixels.
3. **The laws, priority-ordered, numbers first.** Camera law, angle law, composition schema with pixel boxes, light law, style law, palette, an avoid list, and for scenes a verbose inventory of every element.
4. **Output contract.** Exact size, exact destination path, print `GENERATED=<path>`.

![Composition plan 9: slab vertices as pixel coordinates, checkpoint dots along each edge, doctrine panels.](./composition9.webp)

Before a fleet flies, the order itself goes on trial. The draft plus its attachments go to a hostile reviewer with one brief: *break this before the model does*. Those reviews convicted real orders of generation-ruining contradictions: a keyboard both mandated in the inventory and banned in the avoid list, a palette law that excluded its own mandated background color, a diagonal ban no character with hair could satisfy. Each finding costs one review. Each miss costs a fleet.

Verbosity is a dial, not a virtue. Scene orders need inventory-grade transcription or the model drifts identity. Pose edits on isolated characters need three sentences, and every extra sentence degrades them. The same contractor wants opposite briefings for different jobs, and learning which is which cost real fleets.

![One round, one war board: probes, references and verdict captions in a single gallery tab.](./war-board.webp)

## Attachments beat adjectives

The campaign's deepest discovery is a hierarchy: **what you attach moves the model more than what you write**. Ranked by measured power:

1. **Corrective attachment.** Attach the model's own rejected render labeled "approved content, rejected geometry: you drew 62 degrees, the law is 50". The only instrument that ever bent geometry.
2. **Layout-lock repaint.** Attach an approved render as "the layout is CLOSED, your only task is the paint". Shrinks the degrees of freedom to the one axis still wrong.
3. **Drawn law.** A rendered diagram beats the same rule as text, every time.
4. **Canvas orientation.** A 1536x1024 canvas pulls every stall wide no matter what the text says. The medium is an instrument.
5. **World-camera gospel.** Attach the approved neighbor stall: "copy the camera, zero content".
6. **Character gospel.** Attach the film still: "copy the kid, zero camera". Models can split a reference into channels if the order names the channels.
7. **Verbal geometry.** Coordinates and angles in prose. Necessary, never sufficient.

The proof is the console slab. Rules-only variants sat at 62 degrees across a whole round. The *corrective attachment* moved it to 55.5, and a second corrective round reached acceptance. **Convergence by self-reference**, measured, repeatable:

## The wars

**The width war** ran eleven rounds and never fell to language. Frame guides lost. Measure clauses lost: "measure before returning" is unexecutable by an image model. Proportional coverage guards lost.

<Postmortem
  error='Stalls render 15 to 70% wider than ordered, round after round, on every landscape canvas.'
  insight='The canvas composes. Orientation outranks prose, and aspect is unfixable in post: scaling preserves it.'
  fix='Generate stalls on a 1024x1536 portrait canvas. The page refuses to compose a wide stall.'
/>

**The angle war** ended when I stopped describing and vectorized. The platform slab became an SVG: top face a symmetric trapezoid with interior corners 130/50/50/130, front face a plain 90-degree rectangle, sides sloping exactly *68 pixels inward per 81 down*. Coprime integers on purpose: any rounded restatement gets flagged as not-the-authority. Orders carried the six vertices as absolute canvas coordinates, checkpoint dots every 20 rows, and a verdict card pairing the legal construction with the crossed-out previous failure and its measured numbers. **Naming the failure's own numbers is what moved the needle.**

![The slab law as vectors: interior corners 130/50/50/130, sides exactly 68:81.](./slab-angles.webp)

![The verdict card: the legal construction beside the crossed-out 62-degree failure with its measured numbers.](./angle-verdict.webp)

**The Ed war** flew thirty strategies to transplant one character camera. Two won: silhouette-fill and place-this-exact-character. Then I rejected all thirty, because what I wanted was the full stall master, not an isolated Ed. Confirm the deliverable unit before you fly thirty jobs. The supervisor logged it as a scoping failure, which it was.

![The thirty Ed-war strategies on one board: thirty ways to move a camera, two that worked, zero that shipped.](./ed-war-30.webp)

**The stall war** is the one from the cold open, and its autopsy is the paper's title.

**The console endgame** stacked every instrument: slab doctrine, corrective convergence, a *layout lock* that finally flattened the paint, then a page-scale check that ruled the winner 1.81x wider than its neighbors, then portrait probes, then a lamp-post sign redesign from a real reference. The shipped master, gen11-a, fails two of the mechanical checks: aspect 0.92 instead of the family target, measured slope 61. I shipped it anyway. **Numbers make the conversation precise. The eye is the gate.**

## One legal plane

Diffusion models default to perspective. The bazaar needs a flat, authored, game-like projection, and getting one took fourteen rounds and ended user-vectorized. The shipped doctrine: **flat storefront elevation**, camera dead-on at standing eye height, no vanishing points, no room interiors, no side walls. Tall furniture obeys the *two-surface law*: a front rectangle plus one thin horizontal top band, and a visible side face is a rejection. Depth between objects is overlap only. Every vertical is dead vertical.

![The first drawn law: the green legal construction beside the red crossed-out isometric. Attached to every scene order since round 13.](./angle-law.webp)

The slab is the compromise that reconciles "flat like the games stall" with "the rug must tilt toward the viewer". The world stays a flat elevation, but each stall stands on a shallow platform whose top face is the one legal foreshortened plane, bounded, vectorized, checkpointed. **Give the model exactly one plane to be deep in** and it stops inventing cameras. Floor props copy the slab construction. Constructed diagonals are only ever the 68:81 sides. Organic lines are exempt, because an early absolute diagonal ban made orders unsatisfiable: the review flagged that a bent pole and spiky hair were illegal under it.

![The world-camera authority: the games stall, flat storefront, floor as a thin strip.](./games-composed.webp)

The games stall is the camera authority, and its winning phrasing is canon: "a FLAT STOREFRONT seen dead-on, NOT a room, NOT a stage, NOT a diorama, the floor is a THIN HORIZONTAL STRIP". When a new stall's order contradicts that language, every render fails identically, and you already know where the bug is. **Winning words travel verbatim across assets.**

## Flatness is a palette count

Diffusion output is smooth by nature: gradients, stipple, mottle. The bazaar look is the opposite, and the style block that enforces it evolved over eight rounds into canon: "authored low-res 16-bit pixel art, large flat bounded color regions, chunky square clusters, near-black outlines, exactly three tones per material, sparse hard highlights, LOW DETAIL." When renders kept weaving anyway, the *anti-texture law* joined it: zero stipple, zero noise, blocks never smaller than 4x4 px, rust as one or two flat patches, a dead screen as one dark tone plus one flat glare band.

Palettes are **sampled, never invented**: a 16-level bucketed dominant-color scan of the approved authority image, because exact-hex counting on diffusion output ranks outlines first. Every order clamps to the sampled set: "use ONLY these colors, merge strays into the nearest listed value". The console war drove its clamp from 24 hexes to 15. ## Animation that never redraws

Three dead ends, then the architecture.

<Postmortem
  error='Whole-frame edits ("same image, arm raised") re-stage the entire composition: 17 to 57% of pixels drift. Patch edits fix registration at 0.2 to 0.8%, then hallucinate content: a moustache appeared.'
  insight='Diffusion cannot edit less than everything it touches. Registration and identity fail on different axes, so no single edit mode fixes both.'
  fix='Decompose every stall into layers and never redraw the composition: a plate that never changes, effect loops, prop layers, a character layer.'
/>

![The same hover edit, measured twice: whole-frame drift on the left, patch-window drift on the right. Red marks changed pixels.](./drift-diff.webp)

The prime invariant makes the architecture testable: **the rest composite is byte-identical to the approved static**, asserted by script, with a self-healing pass that joins orphan pixels to the nearest layer. "The animation broke the art" stopped being a review argument and became a failing assertion. Where a character overlaps plate furniture, those pixels become named occluder layers so the keeper animates behind the counter while the composite stays byte-true at rest.

Poses are diffusion on the flattened character alone, on chroma, three-sentence orders. Chaining each frame from the previous held identity for a while, then compounded: every chained leg re-diffuses the sprite, saturation drifts, detail mushes. The *sheet doctrine* ended it. Poses are now **one generation**: frame 0 attached as the only asset, the order asks for a four-cell sprite-sheet row whose first cell is an exact copy of frame 0, all poses share one diffusion pass and one palette. Slice by column gaps, register to the rest baseline. Start from the good one, never send the ones-to-fix back.

<Pull>Start from the good one, never send the ones-to-fix back.</Pull>

The smallest trick is my favorite. The idle head-bob is an *implant*: copy the rest frame, shift only the head region down one pixel, leave the body planted. The one-pixel overlap at the neck swallows the seam, the one-pixel void at the hair top reveals dark wall, and the feet never move. Zero new colors, zero diffusion. The failed alternative, bobbing the whole layer, detaches the feet and the character floats.

At runtime every frame of every layer mounts once as absolutely-stacked images and animation flips `opacity` on timers. An earlier src-swapping runtime generated **over 8000 network requests** and flashed mid-decode. Hidden breakpoint trees do not animate, hover greetings hold until leave, and `prefers-reduced-motion` renders final poses statically.

## Eight strangers, one market

Generating a good stall is half the job. Making eight independently generated sprites sit in one architecture without reading as stickers consumed as many rounds as the art.

<Postmortem
  error='The official keyer greyed the w98 violets and turned the games stall half transparent. Clean sprites on white shipped with tinted rims.'
  insight='Despill color metrics execute hues, not intentions, and diffusion always blends art into the key: a 1-2px tinted rim survives any threshold.'
  fix='Key without despill, resolve partial alpha geometrically (edges to 0, interiors to 255), then verify every sprite composited on a hostile color, never on white.'
/>

Scale next. Canvas pixels are **not comparable across generations**: bake scales ranged 0.49 to 0.59, so every cross-stall argument happens in display pixels. Character heights are the calibration instrument: seated Ed displays at 170px, the same as a standing games kid, and the w98 robot tops the family at 294. The editor then applied per-stall tune factors, console times 0.86, travel times 1.05: *the eye correcting the last five percent*.

![Eight stalls at family display scale, bottom aligned. Canvas pixels lie, display pixels do not.](./stall-lineup.webp)

Grounding last. Stall feet plant on a painted walkway strip that overlaps their base, contact shadows are CSS ellipses so they survive repositioning, and the beams occlude stall edges by law. The enabling refactor was **one absolute stacking context per floor**: before it, sibling bands ate z-indexes silently; after it, negative z goes behind stalls and every value works globally. An IntersectionObserver keeps the most visible floor bright and dims the rest. *Depth of field by attention.*

## Ninety-two props, zero backgrounds

Baked backgrounds die in responsive layouts, so the world's furniture is **ninety-two individually keyed sprites**: graffiti with in-jokes, pachinko, a torii gate, a ramen machine, server towers, grow lamps, the sign family. Props are placeable forever, which is why v5 could unplug them in one move.

The recipe that worked: 1536x1024 sheets with pixel-boxed cells, one prop per cell, sliced by script. Per-cell palettes sampled from the previous approved round and capped at *eight hexes per prop*, after "all of them have many colors" rejected an entire fleet. A front-facing law per cell, because cylinders drift isometric. A completeness law with max-footprint numbers, because croppers recur: the toolbox, the standee, the hung bicycle all came back cut before the law. And the *lamp law*: the emitter is lit, the halo hugs the glass, but no projected light in the sprite. **Projection belongs to CSS.**

![The 92-sprite decoration inventory: graffiti, pachinko, torii gate, ramen machine, server towers, grow lamps.](./prop-inventory.webp)

Two props refused diffusion and were coded deterministically: the wall recess, because symmetry kept breaking, and the wall-floor tiles, spliced from the approved tile's measured floor strip under new walls, seams fixed by column-luminance trimming. That pipeline also caught two variant files shipped content-swapped, **by luminance fingerprint, not by eye**. And the contractor works review too: fed the manifest and the stall x-ranges and asked for at most six placement adjustments as JSON, it returned six, five of which were true hits. Reviewer, not oracle. Verify each suggestion against coordinates before applying.

## The street is its own animal

The street is the front door and the only floor that is not market architecture. Its restyle taught the *fidelity lesson*: every regeneration risks losing an approved property, so orders name what must survive. The first round lost the bus's rust and the magenta neon reflection on the alley wall. The second lost the chunky limited colors. The orders grew a keep-list: "magenta side-wall glow mandatory", "rust as flat two-tone patches". Some assets never landed a better version, and **keeping the originals is a ruling too**.

The tileable floor is the street's best pipeline story. Generated edges never matched, so no single image would tile. The fix: a horizontal rail law, a wider canvas at the exact 1536 size, then a continuation generation with a **500-pixel frozen overlap**, stitched at the column of minimum difference, about 2 units of error per pixel. One 1920 strip with no visible seam, `repeat-x`, forever.

Structurally the street predates the market's unit system and keeps its own: measured building widths, percent-anchored overlays, a door and neon baked into the host building's geometry. Porting it onto the market layout system broke it instantly, so it ships as a **sealed component** wrapped in its own variable context. Its motion density is deliberate: bus flip, neon stutter, alley signs, the car driving through. *Arrival should feel busier than browsing.*

## Boxes first, art second

v4 looked right and measured wrong. The audit that killed it had numbers, and the numbers were not close:

<Postmortem
  error='Three disagreeing container widths in one view. Floor-3 gaps of 89, 12.7 and 2.2px at 1680w. No regime between 700 and 1400px. A stairs sprite squashed 3.9x by a preflight img rule.'
  insight='Hand-placed absolute coordinates made every resize a lie. Decorating before validating means auditing never.'
  fix='A one-knob layout system proven in empty boxes from 500 to 1900px, with live rulers asserting gap equality, before a single sprite rode it.'

<Pull>Hand-placed absolute coordinates made every resize a lie.</Pull>

The rebuild started with a spec, in my own notation, ratified before any code:

```
_  = gap, dynamic, ALL EQUAL within a floor
x  = undefined leftover        |  = screen edges
sc/ec = 1400px container       a = stall   S/SM = stairs desktop/mobile

>=1400    |xS_sc_a_a_a_ecx|
700..1400 |S_a_a_a_|
<700      |SM_a_|   one floor = ONE SM + TWO stalls, one per story
          |SM_a_|
```

Then a standalone prototype that draws dashed rulers between every box: green when all gaps on a floor are equal within 1.5px, red when broken, amber for stairs crop with a percentage. Verified from 500 to 1900px with headless Chrome reading real geometry.

![The layout prototype at 1000px: dashed rulers between every box, green means equal gaps within 1.5px.](./proto-b1000.webp)

The whole system hangs off one knob. Everything else derives:

```css
.scene {
  /* THE knob: px per sim unit at the cap */
  --su-cap: 0.9;
  --vstar: 1690;
  /* S soft crop ramp, 1200 -> 700: reclaimed width grows stalls */
  --shift: clamp(0px, (1200px - 100vw) * 0.132, 66px);
  --su: min(
    calc(var(--su-cap) * 1px),
    calc((100vw + var(--shift)) * var(--su-cap) / var(--vstar))
  );
  --s-w: calc(var(--su) * 324);
  --floor-h: calc(var(--su) * 597);
}
```

That is the shipped v5 stylesheet. `--vstar` is **derived, not chosen**: the 1400px container plus one gap plus half the stairs must fit, and the binding floor is the two-stall one. Above 1690px the stalls live in a real container with n+1 equal gaps. Below it the layout goes full bleed and the stairs slide offscreen on a clamp ramp, capped at exactly half their width, and the reclaimed width feeds back into `--su` so it grows stalls, not gaps. *The constants came out of solving that fixpoint, not out of tweaking.*

![The same prototype at 650px: one mobile stairwell spanning two stories, four gaps equal by construction.](./proto-m650.webp)

## The editor lives in the page

Pixel-perfect placement of AI assets needs a human hand at the end, and every external tool edits a copy. The bazaar's layout is CSS math over live viewport units, so **only the page renders the truth**. The editor mounts as a dev overlay on the real route and manipulates inline styles on the real DOM while you watch exactly what ships.

The details that matter: picking selects the *smallest target under the pointer*, collecting tagged ancestors via `document.elementsFromPoint` and sorting by area, so occluded props stay selectable behind bigger boxes. Scaling uses eight handles, corners locked to aspect, edges one axis, with sensitivity damped so a full element-width of drag changes scale by 40%. Transform origin stays bottom-center: **things sit on floors**. Anything can anchor to anything, and anchored props survive relayouts by moving with their stall.

Export puts JSON on the clipboard in sim units, measured band-locally against the floor's stage element, because early viewport-relative exports drifted. The export is **intent, not absolutes**. The standing order was "don't just apply these, update in a harmonious way and responsively", so every editor session got translated into tune factors, gap rules and anchor offsets, never pasted. One unit bridges three worlds: *su* is the asset pipeline's canvas pixels, the stylesheet's calc math, and the editor's screen pixels. **Every drift bug in the project traced back to a second coordinate system.**

## Stairs are the coordinate system

Floor height equals stairs height, 324x597 sim units, aspect never modified: the whole vertical rhythm of the market derives from **one hand-polished sprite**. The mobile stairwell is a separate asset because it must span two stacked stories. It carries exactly two knobs into CSS, both measured from the art: trim aspect 0.464, and the deck walk line at 0.497 of its height, found mechanically as the densest wide band beyond the cage. The story divider sits at the painted deck. **Art wins, layout bends.** Stairs mirror per floor with `scaleX(-1)` so the exits always face the stalls, and the side is DOM order, not flex tricks.

![The mobile stairwell gate: candidates side by side, measured aspects and deck lines, a LAW PASS verdict per probe.](./gate-r23.webp)

The desktop stairs file is sacred in a literal sense: a regeneration once clobbered it mid-polish, and the exporter has carried a skip-existing guard since. **Guardrails live in code, not in memory.**

## Light, baked and projected

Two light systems coexist, and the boundary is a doctrine. **Baked light belongs to the art's story**: the one lit CRT, a neon reflection on the alley wall the fiction owns. **Projected light belongs to the page**: anything that moves, responds or repositions. Every stall order names its single source and enumerates the receivers, because *uncounted light is how diffusion invents lamps*.

The CSS side is the bazaar's second art medium. Editor-placed radial glow spots in seven colors plus black, the black ones running `mix-blend-mode: multiply` as **paintable shadow pools**. Sign halos matched to each asset's own light color and synced to the same keyframes as the sprite swap. A hover wash per stall in its tint, runtime only, never baked into hover frames. And the street neon blinks on an 8-second `steps()` cycle with two stutter clusters, tuned by an explicit ruling: increase the delay between blinks, not the speed. **Motion frequency reads as life.**

One shipped bug earned its law: the papers hologram flickers as a group, a wrapper div carrying the keyframe, because animating the frame images directly fought the opacity-based frame switching. And one screen per stall is lit, white-grey, maximum. Glow is the easiest thing for diffusion to overdo, and *one overdone screen turns a stall into a slot machine*.

## A synthesizer instead of samples

The market is scored by a [237-line synthesizer](https://github.com/sospedra/sospedra.me/blob/master/app/bazaar/sounds.ts). One audio file exists in the whole system, the door slam. Everything else is two primitives: an oscillator with an exponential pitch ramp and decay envelope, and filtered white noise. Even the reverb is procedural, a convolver fed a generated impulse response:

```ts
verb = c.createConvolver()
const len = c.sampleRate * 1.6
const ir = c.createBuffer(2, len, c.sampleRate)
for (let ch = 0; ch < 2; ch++) {
  const data = ir.getChannelData(ch)
  for (let i = 0; i < len; i++) {
    data[i] = (Math.random() * 2 - 1) * (1 - i / len) ** 2.8
  }
}
```

Every stall has a **leitmotif**, a half-second sting on hover matched to its keeper: uses is a 220-to-55Hz sine drop plus a sizzle burst, *a pot hitting a stove*. Games is a three-note square-wave coin-up. Travel is two sine chirps bending like alien small talk. w98 is a pair of descending sweeps, *a machine powering down or up depending on your faith*. The design rules that emerged: synthesis over samples, because **a recorded foley reads like a photo pasted into a drawing**. One sting per encounter, never looped. Wet equals big: reverb marks the door and the floor transitions, dry marks UI. And everything is gesture-gated and opt-in, because sound that autoplays is sound that gets muted.

## The drive in

The bazaar is not a link. The homepage parks a hand-built SVG car on the hero bridge, engine off, and **that parked car is the affordance**. Clicking "bazaar" runs a choreography:

```ts
const duration = isFirstRide
  ? BAZAAR_SIGNATURE_DURATION // 3100ms, the cinematic one
  : BAZAAR_EXPRESS_DURATION // 2500ms, the commute
dispatch({ type: 'depart', duration })
transition.navigateLater('/bazaar', Math.max(duration - 360, 0))
```

The route change fires 360ms before the drive ends, so the page swaps while the car is exiting the viewport and **the cut hides inside the motion**. The first ride each session takes the long signature drive, repeats take the express: ceremony on arrival, speed on habit, tracked by a sessionStorage flag that claims optimistically and falls back to ceremony if storage throws. Reduced motion skips the fiction entirely, because *fiction never blocks access*.

The easing curve lives twice: as a CSS custom property for the transform, and solved numerically in JS so the code knows where the car is at any millisecond. **When CSS and JS must agree about a moving object, share the curve, not the guesses.** On the other side, the street scene runs the same car sprite driving through, and the way back is the bus stop, linked to the homepage. Two vehicles, one loop. The car is about 200 lines of choreography for a three-second joke, and the joke is the thesis: *this is a place you travel to*.

One edge case earned its scar: Next's cache can revive the home page with the car frozen mid-departure, so an effect that notices its second run on the same tree bumps an epoch key and remounts the stage clean. **Page-transition fiction has state**, and state survives navigation in ways plain pages never test.

## The human 10%

"AI-generated" describes the pixels' origin, not the work. Five kinds of human labor bracket every generation.

The sketches came first, pen on paper: the vertical market, the street entry, where each keeper sits and what they hold. **The pipeline never invented a composition.** It executed compositions that already existed on paper, translated into zone contracts and drawn plans, which is exactly why drawn law beats written law inside the pipeline too.

The design language sits above: one midnight city, defined once, spoken in dialects. The bazaar is *the loud room in a quiet house*, the home page keeps a faint skyglow, the papers keep a navy reading band, and deciding what is canon, what is dialect and what is forbidden is classic art direction, none of it delegable.

The Affinity pass comes after: edge repair at sprite scale, palette snapping where clamps leaked, and outright hand-repainting of limbs where chained diffusion drifted. Three shipped files were reworked directly in the assets directory, which is why a law forbids any script from overwriting it. <u>The generated file is a draft. The polished file is the asset.</u>

The software architecture is the fourth kind. Somebody had to decide that every frame mounts once and animation flips opacity, that the layout derives from one knob, that sound is synthesized instead of downloaded, that light is stylesheet math. Those decisions are why a market carrying 230 sprites **scrolls at 60fps and opens under 100ms**. The models paint fast; they do not design runtimes.

And CSS runs beside everything as the second art medium: shadows, glows, reflections masked through a building's own alpha, dimming by attention. It survives relayouts, responds to hover, costs zero image bytes, and iterates at **taste speed instead of regeneration speed**. The honest division of labor: the model produces 90% of the pixels in seconds. The human produces the 10% that makes them shippable, and **100% of the direction**. *The 10% is the slow part. The 100% is the job.*

## The ledger

What twelve days of adversarial art direction actually taught:

1. **Never trust, always measure.** Dimensions, bounding boxes, angles, hashes. Two concurrent jobs once returned the same file. The model's confidence is not evidence.
2. **Attachments beat adjectives.** Corrective attachments beat layout locks beat drawn law beat canvas orientation beat gospels beat prose. Words are the weakest tool in the box.
3. **When every job fails identically, the order is the bug.** Audit your own instructions with the hostility you reserve for the output.
4. **Adversarial review is cheap insurance.** One hostile read of an order costs minutes. One poisoned fleet costs a night.
5. **Freeze what is won.** Close the layout, then fight the paint. One axis of freedom per round.

The rest compresses into a sentence each. The canvas composes, so orientation is a prompt instrument. Verbosity is a dial with opposite settings for scenes and edits. Rule by reference: I never wrote a prompt, I pasted film stills, SVG diagrams and crops with circles of anger, and the supervisor translated taste into contracts. Validate layout in empty boxes before art. Keep one coordinate system. And the shipped console master fails two mechanical checks and is right anyway, because <u>numbers make the conversation precise and the eye wins it</u>.

The method compresses to its name: **adversarial art direction**. Generate under suspicion, approve by eye.
