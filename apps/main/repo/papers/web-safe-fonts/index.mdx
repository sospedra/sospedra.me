In 1985 Apple shipped the [LaserWriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaserWriter). Your font stack still pays for it. Adobe licensed Apple 13 fonts for that machine, and three of them sit in the CSS at the bottom of this page. **Each comma in a font stack sits between an original and its clone.**

The easy story blames the browsers. The real one starts at a printer and ends in a bankruptcy.

In 1984 HP shipped the [HP LaserJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_LaserJet). It was the first laser printer built for mass-market sales. One year later Apple answered with help from Adobe. The LaserWriter carried a built-in PostScript interpreter. With an editor program, **anyone could create, publish and distribute their own content**. That is the [Desktop Publishing revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_publishing).

![The original LaserWriter](laserwriter.jpeg)

## Printers picked the fonts

Adobe licensed Apple the first 13 mass-market fonts. Times, Helvetica, Courier and Symbol. Four variants of each, and one for Symbol. The *LaserWriter Plus* added 22 more later, among them AvantGarde, New Century and Bookman.

Why those fonts? **Technical limits picked them.** Times, Helvetica and Courier print easy. They carry little detail. The printer needs simple roller movements.

Price decided the buyer. Before the revolution IBM sold 600dpi printers at 21.000$. The LaserWriter started at 6.900$.

## Microsoft answered with clones

Now the web. In 1986 Silicon Valley was young and the big fight was about operating systems. A decade later the fight moved to browser control.

The record here is thin. No official documents survive, only a [bunch of declarations from key members](http://www.xnet.se/xpo/typetalk/) of different companies at that time. [Monotype](https://www.monotype.com/) was about to go bankrupt. Monotype drew most of the fonts we use today: Arial, Baskerville, Gothic, Bodoni, Gill. Some of them shipped inside those printers. **Microsoft came to the rescue.**

Monotype then drew a set of analogous fonts for Microsoft. The new fonts copied the licensed ones. A legal war followed, with many parties in it. I do not know every detail of the case. The result is clear. We do not have standard fonts. We have clones.

The clones are the good news. **The Microsoft fonts are almost identical to the original ones.** So name the original first, name the clone second, and the pair holds anywhere. This is the closest the modern world gets to a common font face set. It is also why no list of web-safe fonts exists.

The CSS rules below are safe in any combination of browsers and OS. Embrace them. We need it.

```css
.font-serif {
  font-family: Times, 'Times New Roman', serif;
}

.font-sans {
  font-family: Helvetica, Arial, sans-serif;
}

.font-mono {
  font-family: Courier, 'Courier New', monospace;
}
```

Drop the demand for 100% support and the set widens. The fonts below are the best supported ones. [Almost available everywhere](https://web.mit.edu/jmorzins/www/fonts.html).
