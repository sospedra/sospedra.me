The pseudonym is dying by statute. Since July 2025 the United Kingdom [asks for your face or your passport](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50) before it serves legal adult content. The European Union has spent four years [reviving a proposal to scan private messages](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A52022PC0209). Exchanges photograph your passport for know-your-customer files. The direction is one way.

The demand underneath is legitimate. Fraud drains real accounts. Grooming happens in real chats. Anonymity absolutists answer none of that, so they keep losing. The menu is the problem. It has two items: a surveillance database, or nothing.

I spent a while trying to build the third item the obvious way. Hold the identity, split the key in two, log every opening, anchor the log. Then I attacked it, and it failed in a way that no amount of engineering fixes.

## The thing I got wrong

**A platform that holds your identity cannot prove it did not read it.**

Here is the whole argument. Imagine two worlds. In the first, the platform follows its protocol and publishes a transcript. In the second, it publishes an identical transcript and also kept a copy of your identity. You see the same bytes either way. Nothing you check can tell the two apart.

Splitting the key across two modules does not help, because one company administers both. Logging every use does not help, because a log records what somebody chose to write. Anchoring the log to a blockchain does not help, because anchoring freezes history rather than forcing entries into it.

Cryptography constrains what you can compute. It has nothing to say about whether you computed it quietly.

Forcing publication needs the decryption key to depend on something the platform cannot produce alone, and that only comes into being when the record goes public. Exactly three things qualify. A committee holding shares. Secure hardware whose maker vouches for it. Witness encryption, which would be perfect and does not exist.

## CLAVE

Custody-less accountable verifiable escrow. The name is the design: the platform holds nothing.

1. Your phone reads your passport chip and requires the chip to answer a live challenge, so a copied data dump is not enough.
2. Your phone seals your identity. The platform receives ciphertext and two proofs, and never sees a name.
3. The key that could open it is held by a public threshold network the platform does not run.
4. To open your account, the platform posts a request on a public chain. Only then does the network derive a key, and that key opens **your account and no other**.
5. The network, not the platform, publishes a timelocked note saying which account was opened. It opens on schedule whether the platform cooperates or not.

Silence stops being an option. The platform cannot extend secrecy by doing nothing, because the thing that reveals the truth is a beacon that does not know it exists.

<Aside label='What the platform stores'>
- ciphertext of your identity, openable by nobody it can reach
- a commitment to a random key, never to your name
- a nullifier proving one passport enrolled once, which reveals nothing about which passport
- proofs that all of the above is well formed
- no key material of any kind
</Aside>

## The proof nobody opens

The platform needs to know the ciphertext it stores really does contain a recoverable identity. Otherwise a criminal enrolls with junk and is permanently unopenable.

So the phone shares a random key across 130 ciphertexts, publishes commitments to all of them, and lets a hash pick 26 to open. Those 26 get checked two ways: the share matches its commitment, and the published bytes recompute exactly from the revealed randomness. The remaining 104 still reconstruct.

Opening 26 leaks nothing, because the threshold is 27.

```
soundness[n=130,k=27,o=26]   41.51 bits   b=78   worst-case decryptions 105
pass[b=1]                    8.000e-1
pass[b=78]                   3.185e-13
```

That second pair of numbers is the part worth understanding. A cheat who corrupts one ciphertext gets away with it 80% of the time and **achieves nothing**, because 129 good ones remain. To actually break recovery you need 78 of them, and then your odds are three in ten trillion.

<Aside label='Measured, not estimated'>
- prove on device: 1754 ms
- verify on the platform: 2335 ms
- recover: 2309 ms, using 27 decryptions of a possible 105
- enrollment record: about 30 KB
- cost per registration: between €0.000016 and €0.00082
</Aside>

That last line is the commercial argument. Commercial identity checks run €0.50 to €3.00. This is four to five orders of magnitude cheaper, because the expensive part happens on a chip already in your pocket.

## The two checks you can run yourself

Neither needs expertise.

**Watch the traffic.** Put the app behind a proxy and read what it sends. The app contacts one endpoint and sends one documented message. It does not pin certificates, on purpose, precisely so you can do this.

**Turn off the network.** Sealing works entirely offline. Enroll in airplane mode, then reconnect to send an already-sealed payload. While your identity existed in plaintext, the device had no way to send it anywhere.

## What this does not do

The escrow network could collude. Enough of its nodes together can derive keys with no public request, and nobody would see it. That assumption is the whole foundation, and unlike a platform's private key management, you can at least look at who runs the nodes.

The circuit that proves your passport is real, without revealing it, does not exist yet. The reference implementation evaluates it in the open. That is the honest gap and it is the next thing to build.

The app is still trusted. Reproducible builds, published hashes and network inspection make tampering detectable. Never impossible. Every end-to-end encrypted product in existence has this hole.

And CLAVE detects rather than prevents. A monitor that proves a platform lied has produced a document. Whether that document costs anyone anything is law, not cryptography.

## Where it stands

A reference implementation runs the protocol end to end: enrollment, the escrow proof, key derivation, recovery, the on-chain request, the timelocked disclosure. 31 tests. The settlement layer runs against a mock that refuses to touch a real chain, so iterating costs nothing.

The paper and the code live at [clave.sospedra.me](https://clave.sospedra.me).

Privacy absolutists will read this as a leash. Surveillance advocates will read it as an alarm bolted to their warrant. Both readings are correct, and the discomfort is symmetric on purpose.

The cryptography was the straightforward part. Deciding what not to hold was the rest of it.
